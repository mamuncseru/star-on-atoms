The whole story on one page

Everything in this course hangs on one chain of ideas. Read it once now. You won't understand every step yet, and that's fine: by Session 13 every step will be familiar, and you'll be able to explain this chain in your own words.

  1. Qubits make mistakes. Today's best hardware gets roughly 1 operation in 1,000 wrong. A useful algorithm needs billions of operations.
    Session 1
  2. Error correction spreads one “logical” qubit over many physical qubits, checks them constantly, and fixes mistakes before they pile up. The surface code is the standard way to do it.
    Sessions 2–5
  3. Some gates are easy to do on protected qubits. These are the Clifford gates (H, S, CNOT). On neutral atoms they're especially cheap, because you can do them “transversally”.
    Session 6
  4. But Clifford gates alone can't beat a classical computer. You also need a non-Clifford gate, such as T or a small rotation. The standard way to make one (“magic states”) is very expensive.
    Session 7
  5. STAR is a shortcut for small rotations. It makes a small rotation directly, by trying and keeping only the good attempts. Its error grows with the angle, so tiny angles are cheap.
    Sessions 8–9
  6. QuEra builds machines from neutral atoms. Every hardware operation (moving an atom, a two-atom gate, a measurement) has its own error rate. QuEra's paper lists them in its “Table II”.
    Session 10
  7. Why care: simulating molecules and materials needs millions of small rotations. STAR's error coefficient α decides how big a simulation fits on a machine.
    Session 11

    Sessions 12–13
In one breath

Error correction makes qubits reliable; STAR is a cheap way to add the one kind of gate error correction can't make easily; this project measures which piece of the hardware limits that shortcut.

Check yourself

In two sentences, what is this project about?

QuEra's STAR architecture makes small-angle rotations by post-selection, and its reach is set by one coefficient, α. The project measures which physical noise channel on a neutral-atom machine sets that coefficient, and what halving each channel would buy, using QuEra's own circuits and noise model and an exact calculation tested blind against sampling.

Why do we need error correction at all?

Physical qubits fail about once per thousand operations, and useful algorithms need billions of operations. Error correction encodes one logical qubit in many physical ones and keeps fixing errors, so the logical error rate can be pushed far below the physical one.

What is STAR, in one sentence?

STAR prepares a small logical rotation directly, by rotating a few physical qubits and keeping the attempt only if no error check fires. That skips the expensive T-gate machinery for small angles, at the price of an error that grows with the angle.

Session 1 · 12 min

Errors are Paulis

You already know qubits and gates. The new idea here is how error-correction people describe mistakes. A real qubit can drift in endless ways: its state can tilt a little, or leak energy. Error correction needs only three kinds of mistakes, the Pauli errors.

  • X: a bit flip. It swaps |0⟩ and |1⟩, like flipping a classical bit.
  • Z: a phase flip. It leaves |0⟩ alone and turns |1⟩ into −|1⟩. In the other basis it swaps |+⟩ and |−⟩. Classical bits have no such error.
  • Y: both at once. Y = iXZ, so a Y error is an X error and a Z error together.
An X error moves the state between the poles. A Z error moves it across the equator. Every other small error is a mix of these, and the checks in later sessions “snap” the mix into one of them.

Why three are enough. Any small error on one qubit can be written as a combination of I (no error), X, Y and Z. When error correction measures its checks, the measurement forces the qubit to “pick” one of them, just as measuring a qubit forces it to pick |0⟩ or |1⟩. So if you can fix X and Z, you can fix everything. People call this the digitization of errors.

How we model noise. In simulations, every operation is followed by a random Pauli error with a small probability p. The most common model is depolarizing noise: with probability p something goes wrong, and it is X, Y or Z with equal chance (p/3 each). A typical number is p = 10−3, one mistake per thousand operations. Real hardware isn't equal-chance: neutral atoms make many more Z errors than X errors. We call that a Z-biased noise, and it matters later.

One of X (bit flip), Z (phase flip) or Y (both) hitting a qubit.

The chance that one physical operation goes wrong. Around 10−3 today.
Depolarizing noise

Biased noise
Some Paulis are much more likely than others. Atoms: mostly Z.
In one breath

Every error that matters is X, Z or both; fix those and you've fixed everything, and we model noise as random Paulis with probability p.

Check yourself

What's the difference between an X error and a Z error?

X flips the bit value, |0⟩↔|1⟩. Z flips the phase, |+⟩↔|−⟩, and has no classical counterpart. A quantum code has to catch both, which is why quantum codes need two kinds of checks.

Why is it enough to correct Pauli errors when real errors are continuous?

Any single-qubit error is a linear combination of I, X, Y, Z. Measuring the code's checks projects the error onto one Pauli pattern, so the decoder only ever sees discrete Paulis. Correcting those corrects the continuous error too.

What does “depolarizing noise at p = 10⁻³” mean?

After each operation, with probability 10⁻³ a random Pauli error happens, and X, Y and Z are equally likely, each with 10⁻³/3.

What is biased noise, and which way are neutral atoms biased?

Biased noise means one Pauli is much more likely than the others. On neutral atoms, Z errors dominate, mainly because of how the Rydberg gate works. QuEra's noise model (Table II) reflects that, with bigger Z and ZZ terms than X terms.

Session 2 · 15 min

The repetition code: your first code

The simplest error-correcting code is one you already use in daily life: say it three times. To store one bit, store copies: 0 becomes 00000 and 1 becomes 11111. If one copy flips, the majority still tells you the truth.

Quantum mechanics adds one twist: you may not look at the qubits directly. Measuring a qubit destroys its superposition. So instead of reading the copies, we measure parity checks between neighbours: “are qubits 1 and 2 the same?” A parity check answers that question without revealing whether they are both 0 or both 1. The list of check answers is called the syndrome.

Qubits
Click a qubit (circle) to flip it. Each check (small square) lights up when its two neighbours disagree. The decoder sees only the lit checks and flips the fewest qubits that explain them.

Try this: flip one qubit, then two, then three in a 5-qubit code. With one or two flips the decoder repairs everything. With three neighbouring flips the checks look exactly like two flips on the other side. The decoder “repairs” the wrong way and the stored bit flips. That is a logical error.

The number of copies is the distance d: the smallest number of flips that turns one codeword into the other without any check noticing. A distance-d code corrects up to (d−1)/2 errors. If each qubit fails with small probability p, a logical error needs about d/2 failures at once, so the logical error rate falls roughly like p(d+1)/2. Adding qubits makes the memory exponentially better, as long as p is small.

Why this is not enough for qubits. The repetition code only catches X errors. A Z error on any copy flips the phase of the whole logical state, and no parity check notices. A real quantum code must catch X and Z at the same time. That needs a 2D code, which is Session 4.

The protected qubit, stored across many physical qubits.

In one breath

Store copies, compare neighbours instead of reading them, and let a decoder fix the fewest flips that explain the comparisons; it fails only when about half the copies fail at once.

Check yourself

What is a syndrome?

The pattern of parity-check outcomes. It tells you where errors probably are without telling you anything about the encoded information, so measuring it doesn't disturb the logical state.

Why can't we just measure each qubit to check it?

Measuring a qubit collapses its superposition and destroys the information we're protecting. Parity checks measure only “same or different”, which reveals errors but not the data.

What is the code distance, and how many errors can a distance-5 code correct?

The smallest number of physical errors that changes the logical state without being detected. A distance-d code corrects up to (d−1)/2 errors, so distance 5 corrects 2.

Why does the repetition code fail as a quantum memory?

It only detects bit flips. A single phase flip on any copy flips the logical phase, and none of its checks notice. You need a code with two kinds of checks, like the surface code.

How does the logical error rate scale with distance?

Roughly as p to the power (d+1)/2, because a logical error needs about half the qubits to fail together. So for small p, each step up in distance multiplies the protection.

Session 3 · 15 min

Measuring a check, and why we repeat it

How do you measure a parity without looking? With a helper qubit, the ancilla. Start the ancilla in |0⟩. Do a CNOT from each data qubit in the check onto the ancilla. Each CNOT adds that data qubit's value to the ancilla, so the ancilla ends up holding the sum (mod 2) of the data values: the parity. Measure only the ancilla. The data qubits are never measured.

A Z-type check (does the pair agree in the 0/1 basis?) uses CNOTs from data to ancilla. An X-type check is the same circuit turned around: ancilla in |+⟩, CNOTs from ancilla to data, measure in the X basis. Neutral atoms do each CNOT as a CZ between two Hadamards.

Measurements fail too. The ancilla measurement itself can give the wrong answer. So we never trust a single round. We measure every check again and again: one pass over all the checks is a round. A surface code of distance d usually runs about d rounds per logical step.

Detectors. The useful signal is a change: “did this check's answer change since the last round?” Each such comparison is a detector. If nothing went wrong, every detector reads 0. The picture below is the most useful one in error correction. Time runs upward, checks run across, and each dot is a detector.

Left: an error on a data qubit flips the two checks beside it from that round on, so two neighbouring detectors light up in the same round (a space-like pair). Right: a wrong measurement flips one check for one round only, so the same check lights up in two consecutive rounds (a time-like pair).

This is why decoders work in space-time: they see dots in a 3D picture (two space directions plus time) and must explain them as pairs. It's also why one fault usually lights exactly two detectors. Remember that “two”: it matters in Session 5, and it is the heart of the bug this project reported to tsim.

Post-selection. Sometimes we don't try to fix anything. If any detector lights, we throw the whole attempt away and start again. That's post-selection. It's only sensible for short, repeatable tasks like preparing a special state, and it costs time: the fraction of attempts you keep is the acceptance rate. STAR (Session 8) relies on it.

In one breath

An ancilla collects a parity through CNOTs; we repeat the checks every round because measurements fail too, and a detector fires when a check changes, so one fault usually lights two detectors.

Check yourself

What's an ancilla used for?

To measure a stabilizer without measuring the data. CNOTs copy the parity of the data qubits onto the ancilla, and only the ancilla is measured.

What is a detector, and why compare rounds?

A detector is the XOR of a check's outcome with its previous outcome (or with a known starting value). It's deterministic without errors, so any 1 signals a fault. Comparing rounds separates data errors from measurement errors.

How do a data error and a measurement error look different?

A data error lights two neighbouring detectors in the same round, a space-like pair. A measurement error lights the same check in two consecutive rounds, a time-like pair.

What is post-selection, and what does it cost?

Throwing away an attempt whenever any detector fires, instead of correcting. It gives very clean outputs, but it costs repetitions: if the acceptance rate is 20%, you need five attempts on average.

Session 4 · 20 min

The surface code

The surface code is the repetition code grown into two dimensions, so it can catch X and Z errors at the same time. It's the code QuEra's STAR circuits use, so this session is the most important one to get comfortable with.

  • Data qubits sit on a d × d grid (the circles below).
  • Checks sit on the squares between them, in two colours like a chessboard. A blue square is an X-check: it measures the product of X on its corner qubits. An amber square is a Z-check: the product of Z on its corners.
  • On the edges there are half-checks that touch only two qubits.

The one rule to remember: a check catches the errors it doesn't commute with. X and Z anticommute. So an X-check catches Z errors, and a Z-check catches X errors. A check fires when it sees an odd number of the errors it catches.

Play with it, and notice three things.

  1. One error lights its neighbours. A Z error in the middle fires the two blue X-checks beside it.
  2. A chain lights only its two ends. Put Z errors on a line of neighbours: the checks in the middle see two errors (even), so they stay quiet. Only the ends fire. The decoder sees two dots and must guess the path between them.
  3. A chain from one edge to the other lights nothing. A full column of Z errors fires no check at all, yet it changes the stored state. That column is the logical Z operator, ZL. Likewise a full row of X is XL.

The shortest such invisible chain has d qubits, so the code distance is d. A distance-d patch uses d2 data qubits plus d2−1 ancillas, one per check: 17 qubits at d = 3, 49 at d = 5, 97 at d = 7.

Why everybody uses it. Every check touches at most four neighbouring qubits, it works on a flat 2D chip, it tolerates relatively high error rates (about 1%, Session 5), and fast decoders exist. Its weakness is that only a few gates are easy on it (Session 6).

Stabilizer
Another name for a check operator. The code's states are the ones every stabilizer leaves unchanged.

Logical operator
A chain from one boundary to the opposite one. It fires no check but changes the logical state.

The edges of the patch. A Z chain can end on the top or bottom edge without firing anything; an X chain can end on the left or right edge.
In one breath

Qubits on a grid, X-checks and Z-checks on the squares between them; an error chain fires only its ends, and a chain that crosses the whole patch fires nothing and is a logical error, so the distance is the width d.

Check yourself

What do X-type and Z-type stabilizers detect?

X-type stabilizers anticommute with Z errors, so they detect Z (phase) errors. Z-type stabilizers detect X (bit) errors. A Y error is both, so it can fire both kinds.

How many physical qubits does a distance-5 surface code patch use?

25 data qubits plus 24 ancillas, one per check: 49 in total. In general d² + (d² − 1) = 2d² − 1.

What is a logical operator in the surface code?

A string of Paulis running from one boundary to the opposite one. It commutes with every check, so it fires nothing, but it acts on the encoded qubit. The logical Z is a column of Z's; the logical X is a row of X's. Its length, d, is the code distance.

Why does a chain of errors only light its endpoints?

Each check in the middle of the chain overlaps two errors. Two anticommuting errors commute overall, so the check doesn't fire. Only the checks at the ends see a single error.

Why is the surface code the default choice?

It needs only nearest-neighbour checks of weight four in 2D, it has a high threshold of about 1%, and fast matching decoders exist. Its downside is that only some logical gates are cheap on it.

Session 5 · 15 min

Decoding, the threshold, and hyperedges

The decoder's job. After some rounds you have a set of lit detectors in space-time. Since most faults light exactly two detectors, the decoder pairs the lit detectors up (or pairs one with the boundary), choosing the pairing with the shortest total length. Shortest means “fewest faults”, the most likely explanation. This is minimum-weight perfect matching (MWPM), and PyMatching is the standard fast implementation. If the chosen paths plus the real errors happen to form a chain across the patch, the result is a logical error.

The threshold. Here is the most important curve in error correction. Below a certain physical error rate, the threshold (about 1% for the surface code), making the code bigger makes the logical error rate drop fast. Above it, a bigger code is worse, because there are more places for errors. A rule of thumb:

pL ≈ 0.1 × (p / pth)(d+1)/2

Drag the slider. Left of the threshold (dashed line), each step up in distance multiplies the protection by about pth/p. Right of it, bigger codes lose. This is the rule-of-thumb formula above, not a simulation.

At p = 10−3 (ten times below threshold), each step from d to d+2 makes the logical error rate about 10 times smaller. That's why hardware groups fight so hard for every factor in p.

Hyperedges, and a decoder trap

Not every fault lights exactly two detectors. A Y error in the bulk lights two X-checks and two Z-checks: four detectors at once. Some two-qubit gate faults do the same. A fault that lights more than two detectors is called a hyperedge. Matching only understands pairs, so such a fault must be decomposed into pairs first, for example Y = X + Z, each part lighting its own pair.

Here is what this project found (Session 12 has the tool names). QuEra's challenge repository shows a reference curve for its STAR circuits. That curve is reproduced only if you hand PyMatching tsim's default error model, which is not decomposed. PyMatching then silently ignores every hyperedge: 421 of 706 fault types at d = 3, about 19% of the fault probability. Decoded with a decomposed model, the logical error rate is about 9 times lower. It was reported as tsim issue #232, and at the maintainers' request pull request #234 adds a warning and makes decompose_errors=True work.

Check yourself

What is the threshold, and why does it matter?

The physical error rate below which increasing the code distance lowers the logical error rate. Below it, logical errors fall exponentially with distance; above it, bigger codes are worse. For the surface code it's about 1% with circuit-level noise.

At p = 10⁻³, how much does going from d = 3 to d = 5 help?

With p/p_th ≈ 0.1, the rule of thumb p_L ∝ (p/p_th)^((d+1)/2) gives 0.1² at d = 3 and 0.1³ at d = 5, so about ten times fewer logical errors.

How does a matching decoder work?

It treats each fault as an edge between the two detectors it lights, weighted by how unlikely it is. Given the lit detectors, it finds the pairing with minimum total weight, the most likely set of faults, and applies the corresponding correction.

What's a hyperedge, and what goes wrong if you ignore it?

A fault that flips more than two detectors, such as a Y error. Matching handles only pairs, so hyperedges must be decomposed. PyMatching silently drops undecomposed hyperedges, so the decoder doesn't know about those faults and fails more often.

What did the project find in tsim's error model?

Reproducing QuEra's ETH QHack STAR curve, the project found it matches only when PyMatching is given tsim's default, undecomposed error model, which silently drops faults flipping more than two detectors, about 19% of the fault probability at d = 3. With a decomposed model the logical error rate is about nine times lower. It was reported to tsim with a reproducer (issue #232), and at the maintainer's request a pull request (#234) adds a docstring warning and support for decompose_errors=True.

Session 6 · 15 min

Gates on protected qubits: transversal gates and color codes

A memory isn't a computer. We need gates on logical qubits, and we can't decode, apply the gate, and re-encode: the unprotected moment would ruin everything. The cleanest trick is a transversal gate: do the same physical gate on every qubit of the block, or pairwise between two blocks (qubit 1 with qubit 1, qubit 2 with qubit 2, and so on).

A transversal CNOT between two surface-code patches: nine physical CNOTs, each joining matching qubits. No physical gate touches two qubits of the same block, so one fault can damage at most one qubit per block, which the code can fix.

Why transversal means safe. Errors spread through two-qubit gates. A transversal gate never connects two qubits inside the same block, so a single fault stays a single error per block, and the code corrects it. You get fault tolerance for free.

Which gates can be transversal?

  • Surface code: CNOT between two patches is transversal. H and S are not directly (they need extra tricks).
  • Color code: a 2D code drawn on faces coloured red, green and blue, where every face is both an X-check and a Z-check. Because the X-checks and Z-checks have the same shape, H (which swaps X and Z) is transversal, and so is S. So the whole Clifford group (H, S, CNOT) is transversal. The smallest color code is the 7-qubit Steane code, drawn below.
The 7-qubit color code. Each coloured face has four qubits and is both an X-check and a Z-check. Same shape for both kinds of check is what makes H transversal.

The catch: Eastin–Knill. A theorem says no code can make a universal set of gates transversal. Some gate always has to come from somewhere else. For the surface and color codes, that missing gate is a non-Clifford one, such as T. Session 7 is about it.

Why atoms love transversal gates. On a neutral-atom machine you can pick up a whole patch of atoms, move it next to another patch, and fire one laser pulse that does a CZ on every pair at once. A transversal CNOT is one move and one pulse. QuEra's architecture is built around this, together with “correlated decoding”, which decodes several blocks together and needs about one round of checks per logical gate instead of d.

Check yourself

What is a transversal gate, and why is it fault-tolerant?

It applies the same physical gate to each qubit of a block, or pairwise between blocks. No physical gate couples two qubits of the same block, so a single fault produces at most one error per block, which the code corrects.

What's special about the color code?

Its X-checks and Z-checks live on the same faces with the same shape. So H, which swaps X and Z, is transversal, and so is S. The whole Clifford group is transversal, unlike on the surface code.

What does the Eastin–Knill theorem say?

No quantum error-correcting code has a universal set of transversal logical gates. At least one gate, usually the non-Clifford one, has to be done another way, for example with magic states or STAR.

Why are transversal gates especially attractive on neutral atoms?

Atoms can be moved, so you can bring a whole code block next to another and apply one global Rydberg pulse that performs a CZ on every pair in parallel. A transversal two-qubit logical gate becomes a single move plus a single pulse.

Session 7 · 15 min

The hard gate: T, magic states, and small rotations

Why Clifford gates aren't enough. A circuit made only of Clifford gates (H, S, CNOT) and Pauli measurements can be simulated efficiently on a classical computer. That's the Gottesman–Knill theorem, and it's exactly why the simulator stim can run billions of shots. A quantum computer that can only do Cliffords gives no speed-up. We need at least one non-Clifford gate. The usual choice is T = diag(1, eiπ/4), which is a Z-rotation by π/4.

Gate teleportation. T isn't transversal on the surface code. Instead, prepare a special state, the magic state |T⟩ = T|+⟩, in a separate logical qubit. Then a CNOT, a measurement, and (depending on the outcome) an S correction apply T to your data. Each T gate uses up one magic state. The correction has to wait for the measurement and the decoder, a delay called the reaction time.

Teleporting a T gate. The same circuit with a different input state teleports any Z-rotation. That's how STAR's states are used too (Session 8).

Making magic states clean. A freshly prepared magic state has an error about as large as p, far too big. Two ways to clean it:

  • Distillation: take 15 noisy magic states, run a small code's checks, post-select, and get 1 much better state: error 35p3. At p = 10−3 that's 3.5 × 10−8. It works, but the “factories” that do it take many qubits and much time.
  • Cultivation (2024): grow one magic state inside a small color code, checking it again and again and post-selecting. It reaches very low error at a cost close to a single CNOT.

The real problem: small rotations. Simulating physics needs Z-rotations by arbitrary small angles, RZ(θ), millions of them. With only Cliffords and T, each rotation must be approximated by a long sequence, about 3 log2(1/ε) T gates for accuracy ε. At ε = 10−10 that's about 100 T gates, each needing its own magic state, for one small rotation.

Rough order of magnitude for one small rotation. STAR's attempts are cheap, but its result isn't perfect: its error grows with the angle, which is the subject of Session 9.

Check yourself

Why do we need non-Clifford gates?

By Gottesman–Knill, circuits of Clifford gates and Pauli measurements are efficiently classically simulable. Universality, and any quantum advantage, needs at least one non-Clifford gate, such as T.

What is a magic state and how is it used?

A state like T|+⟩. You consume it by gate teleportation: a CNOT with the data, a measurement, and a Clifford correction depending on the outcome. The net effect applies T to the data.

What does 15-to-1 distillation do?

It takes 15 noisy magic states with error p, measures the checks of a small code, post-selects, and outputs one state with error about 35p³. It's reliable but expensive in qubits and time.

Why are small-angle rotations expensive in standard fault tolerance?

An arbitrary rotation must be synthesized from Cliffords and T gates, about 3 log₂(1/ε) T gates for accuracy ε, so roughly 100 T gates, each needing a magic state, for one rotation. Simulations need millions of rotations.

What is the reaction time?

The delay between measuring during a teleported gate and applying the correction, because the decoder must finish first. On neutral atoms, measurement and decoding take on the order of a millisecond, so it sets the clock speed of non-Clifford gates.

Session 8 · 20 min

STAR: a small rotation by post-selection

STAR (space-time efficient analog rotation) makes a small logical rotation directly, without a hundred T gates. You now have every piece you need to understand it.

  1. Start with a surface-code patch holding logical |+⟩.
  2. Pick the qubits of one logical Z string: a column of k qubits (in QuEra's teaching circuits, k = d, the whole column).
  3. Rotate each of them by the same physical angle θ* about Z. Each rotation is RZ(θ*) = c·I − i s·Z, with c = cos(θ*/2) and s = sin(θ*/2).
  4. Measure the checks. Keep the attempt only if nothing fires.

Why that works. Multiply out the k rotations. You get a sum over every way of putting Z on some of the column's qubits: no Z's, Z on one qubit, on two, and so on, up to Z on all of them. Call each term a branch. Now recall Session 4: a partial Z string is a broken chain, so its ends fire X-checks. Every branch with Z on some but not all of the column gets caught. Exactly two branches fire nothing:

  • “no Z at all”, the identity, with amplitude ck;
  • “Z on the whole column”, which is ZL, with amplitude (−is)k.

If we keep only attempts where nothing fired, the patch is left in ck·I + (−is)k·ZL applied to |+⟩. That is a logical Z-rotation, by an angle θ with tan(θ/2) = tank(θ*/2). Raising a small number to the power k makes it much smaller, so a modest physical angle gives a tiny logical one.

Two things to notice. The first surprises most people.

  • Smaller angles are accepted more often. A smaller θ needs a smaller θ*, so the “no Z” branch dominates. At k = 3, about 31% of attempts succeed at π/4, and 86% at π/128.
  • Bigger k is accepted less often. With more rotated qubits there are more ways to break the chain. At π/16: 57% for k = 3, 19% for k = 5, 5.5% for k = 7. That's one reason QuEra's own protocol rotates only k = 3 qubits even in large patches.

Using the state: repeat until success

The prepared state is used by gate teleportation (Session 7's circuit). The measurement outcome is random: half the time the data gets RZ(θ) as wanted, and half the time RZ(−θ), the wrong direction. The fix is to rotate by 2θ next; that again succeeds or overshoots with probability one half, and so on, doubling each time. With θ = π/2n, the chain must end by π/2, which is the Clifford S gate and can be done exactly. On average it takes two attempts.

Check yourself

Explain STAR in thirty seconds.

Take a surface-code patch in logical plus. Rotate each qubit of one logical-Z string by a physical angle θ*. Expanded, that's a sum over Z on every subset of the string. Any partial string fires X-checks, so post-selecting on a quiet syndrome keeps only the identity and the full string, Z_L. Their combination is a logical Z rotation with tan(θ/2) = tan^k(θ*/2). Then you teleport it in, with a repeat-until-success chain for the sign.

Why are partial Z strings rejected?

A partial string is a chain with ends inside the patch. The X-checks at those ends see one Z error each, anticommute, and fire. Only the empty string and the full string, which runs boundary to boundary, fire nothing.

How does the acceptance rate depend on the angle and on k?

It's cos^2k(θ*/2) + sin^2k(θ*/2). Smaller logical angles need smaller physical angles, so acceptance rises: about 31% at π/4 and 86% at π/128 for k = 3. Larger k lowers it sharply: at π/16, 57% for k = 3 against 5.5% for k = 7.

What is the repeat-until-success chain?

Teleporting the rotation gives +θ or −θ with equal probability. After −θ, you apply 2θ to fix it, which again succeeds or overshoots, and so on, doubling. With θ = π/2^n the chain ends at π/2, an exact Clifford S gate. On average it takes two attempts.

Why is STAR called “partially” fault-tolerant?

Its error isn't suppressed exponentially with distance like a fully fault-tolerant gate. Some single faults survive post-selection and leave a wrong angle, so the error is proportional to p times something that shrinks with the angle. That's fine for small angles in the early fault-tolerant regime.

Session 9 · 20 min

Where STAR's error comes from, and what α means

Now add noise. Most faults near the rotation are harmless, because they fire some check, and the attempt is thrown away. The dangerous fault is a Z error on one of the rotated qubits, right after the rotation, before any check has looked at that qubit.

Why it's dangerous: the checks can't tell this fault apart from the rotation itself. They see the fault and the branch together. So with a Z fault on qubit j, the checks stay quiet in two new cases: when the rotation produced “Z on j alone” (the fault cancels it, leaving nothing), and when it produced “Z on everything except j” (the fault completes the full string). Those two branches survive post-selection instead of the intended ones. They also make a rotation, but by the wrong angle, and nothing in the syndrome reveals it.

The “branch flip”. Left: no fault, the intended rotation, tan(θ/2) = tk. Right: one hidden Z on qubit j, and the surviving pair gives tan = tk−2 instead, in the opposite direction (t = tan(θ*/2)).

How big is that error? It's the chance of such a Z fault (proportional to p) times a function of the angle, fk(θ), which combines “how likely the flipped branches survive” and “how wrong their angle is”. For small angles, fk shrinks like θ2−2/k. This is the paper's misrotation term. This project's first big result was that, for these circuits, the whole injection error is one constant times this shape, over a 150–400× range of error rates.

The angle shape of the injection error, computed exactly (the same formula as starlab's branch_flip_shape). The real error is p times a constant times this. Hover or tap a point to read it.

From injection error to α

The paper summarises everything in one headline law: a teleported rotation by θ fails with probability about

pL ≈ α · p · θ

α is a single number that says how sensitive STAR is to noise. It comes from averaging the injection error over the repeat-until-success chain (Session 8). The big angles near the end of the chain (π/4, π/8, π/16) carry most of it.

Why α matters so much. If each rotation by θ costs error αpθ, a computation can afford a total rotation of about 1/(αp) before errors pile up. For a physics simulation, that total angle grows with (number of sites) × (simulated time), the simulation volume. So halve α, and you can simulate twice as much on the same machine.

Connecting to hardware. If one hardware channel (say, single-qubit gate errors) causes a fraction f of STAR's error, then halving that channel's error multiplies α by (1 − f/2). Simulation volume grows by 1/(1 − f/2). At d = 5, single-qubit gates cause 48.5% of the error, so halving them buys ×1.32. That's the project's headline number (Session 13).

Which faults survive post-selection in STAR, and what do they do?

The flipped branches carry weight only when the physical rotation is large enough to populate them, and the misrotation they cause is smaller for small angles. Together the error goes like p · θ^(2 − 2/k) for small θ.

Session 10 · 15 min

Neutral atoms: QuEra's hardware

QuEra's qubits are single atoms (rubidium) held in place by tightly focused laser beams called optical tweezers. Hundreds or thousands of them sit in a 2D array. Each atom's qubit is two of its energy levels, |0⟩ and |1⟩.

A neutral-atom processor, simplified. Atoms are moved between a storage zone and an entangling zone. One global laser pulse gives every close pair a CZ. An atom in the zone with no partner is a spectator: it gets no gate, but it still picks up some error.
  • Single-qubit gates are laser pulses on the atoms.
  • The two-qubit gate (CZ) uses the Rydberg blockade. A laser lifts |1⟩ to a Rydberg state, a hugely swollen atom. Two Rydberg atoms closer than a few microns (5–10 μm) can't both be excited: one blocks the other. A carefully shaped pulse turns that blockade into a CZ gate, with fidelity around 99.5%. One global pulse does this for every close pair at once.
  • Moving atoms. Extra tweezers grab whole rows and columns and drag them (rows can't cross). A move takes a few hundred microseconds. Moving lets any qubit meet any other, and moving a whole patch onto another gives a transversal gate (Session 6).
  • Measurement is a photograph of the atoms' fluorescence, taking about a millisecond. That's slow, which is why waiting on measurements (reaction time) matters.
  • Atom loss. An atom can escape its trap. QuEra's noise model treats a lost atom as a Y error.
  • Z-biased noise. The Rydberg step mostly causes phase (Z) errors, so Z and ZZ errors dominate the CZ's error.

Check yourself

How does a neutral-atom machine do a two-qubit gate?

With the Rydberg blockade. A laser couples |1⟩ to a Rydberg state; two atoms within a few microns can't both be excited, so the pair acts like a single two-level system. A pulse sequence that gives the doubly-excited path a phase of −1 implements a CZ. One global pulse does it for every close pair in parallel, at about 99.5% fidelity.

What is a spectator error?

Error on an atom that sits in the entangling zone during a CZ pulse but has no partner. It doesn't receive a gate, but it still sees the laser and picks up some error, mostly Z. In the project's budget, spectators cause about a quarter of STAR's injection error.

Why does atom movement matter?

Moving atoms gives flexible connectivity and makes transversal gates a single move plus a pulse. But moves take hundreds of microseconds and add small errors and occasional loss. In the project's budget, moves contribute only about 0.5% of the injection error.

Why are neutral-atom errors biased towards Z?

The dominant errors come from the Rydberg excitation: decay and dephasing during the gate mostly scramble the phase, not the bit value. So Z and correlated ZZ errors are the largest terms in the CZ's error.

What are typical timescales?

A CZ pulse is well under a microsecond, a move takes a few hundred microseconds, and imaging readout about a millisecond. So an error-correction cycle is about a millisecond, and anything that waits for a measurement pays that.

Session 11 · 12 min

Why all this: simulating physics

The first useful job for an early fault-tolerant machine is likely to be simulating quantum physics: how electrons move in a material, or how a molecule reacts. The workhorse example is the Fermi–Hubbard model: electrons hop between neighbouring sites of a lattice (strength t) and repel each other when two share a site (strength U). It's simple to write and very hard to solve classically. QuEra's own STAR papers aim at it.

Trotterization. We want to apply e−iHt. The Hamiltonian is a sum of simple pieces, H = A + B, but they don't commute, so we can't apply them separately in one go. Instead we chop time into r small steps and alternate: (e−iAΔt e−iBΔt)r. The error comes from the pieces not commuting and shrinks as the steps get smaller.

One Trotter step is a layer of small Pauli rotations (one per term of H), repeated r times. Each Pauli rotation reduces to one small RZ(θ) on one qubit, sandwiched between Clifford gates. Those RZ(θ)s are what STAR supplies.

From electrons to qubits. Electrons are fermions, not qubits. The Jordan–Wigner mapping turns “is there an electron on site j?” into qubit j, and turns each hopping term into Pauli strings such as (XX + YY)/2, with Z's in between to track fermion signs. Every term of H becomes a Pauli string P, and every step e−iΔt·P becomes a small rotation.

The link to α. Count the rotations: (number of terms) × (number of Trotter steps), each by a small angle. The total angle grows with the number of sites times the simulated time, the simulation volume. Since STAR can afford a total angle of about 1/(αp), α directly decides how large a material and how long a time you can simulate.

Check yourself

What is Trotterization, and where does its error come from?

Approximating e^(−iHt) for H = A + B by alternating short steps of e^(−iAΔt) and e^(−iBΔt), r times. The error comes from A and B not commuting. First-order Trotter error falls like t²/r; the symmetric second-order version falls faster.

Why do small rotations show up in physics simulation?

After Jordan–Wigner, every term of the Hamiltonian is a Pauli string, and each Trotter step applies e^(−iΔt·P), a rotation by an angle of order Δt. A Pauli rotation reduces to a single R_Z between Clifford gates, so the whole simulation is Cliffords plus millions of small R_Z rotations.

How does α limit a simulation?

Each rotation by θ costs error αpθ, so the total rotation the computation can afford is about 1/(αp). The total rotation grows with sites times simulated time, so the simulation volume scales as 1/(αp).

What does Jordan–Wigner do to a hopping term?

It maps c†_i c_j + h.c. to qubit operators. For neighbouring sites in 1D that's (XX + YY)/2 on the two qubits. For non-neighbours, Z's on the qubits in between keep track of fermion signs, which makes the strings long in 2D.

Session 12 · 15 min

The tools: stim, tsim, error models, and an exact count

  • stim simulates Clifford circuits with Pauli noise, extremely fast: billions of shots. It can't simulate a general rotation like STAR's RZ(θ*), because that isn't Clifford (Gottesman–Knill again).
  • tsim is QuEra's sampler for circuits that are almost Clifford: a few non-Clifford gates, like STAR's rotations. It samples every number in the project's protocols.
  • PyMatching is the matching decoder from Session 5. It needs an error model whose faults are split into pairs.

The detector error model (DEM) is the most useful object in this whole field. It is a list: for every possible fault, its probability, which detectors it lights, and whether it flips the logical result (written L0). stim builds it automatically from a noisy circuit.

# probability   detectors it lights     flips the result?

error(0.00013)  D1 D2 D4 D7              # a hyperedge: four detectors
error(0.00013)  D1 D4 ^ D2 D7            # the same fault, decomposed into two pairs
A few lines of a detector error model. Decoders read this list instead of the circuit.

The Clifford proxy. Set the STAR rotation angle to zero, so every RZ becomes the identity. The circuit is now Clifford, and stim can build its error model. The exact count lives there.

The exact count, in plain words (starlab/enumerate.py, about 100 lines):

  1. Work out which detectors each STAR branch lights in the proxy. A branch is Z on some subset of the rotated qubits.
  2. Go through every fault in the proxy's error model. A fault survives post-selection only if it lights exactly the same detectors as some branch, because then the two cancel and the checks see nothing.
  3. For each surviving fault, its error is its probability times a closed-form weight for that branch pair, the fk(θ) of Session 9.
  4. Add them up. That is the injection error, exactly, to first order in p, with no sampling and no fitted parameter.

Pre-registration. Every experiment in this project was written down, with its predictions and pass/fail rule, and committed to git before it ran. A guard script fails the build if a result is older than its protocol. That's how you can say the blind test was really blind.

Check yourself

What is a detector error model?

A list of independent fault mechanisms, each with a probability, the set of detectors it flips, and the logical observables it flips. stim builds it from a noisy circuit, and decoders such as PyMatching are built from it.

Why can't stim simulate STAR directly?

stim simulates only Clifford circuits. STAR's physical rotations by θ* are non-Clifford, so you need a sampler like tsim, or, for the error model, the θ = 0 Clifford proxy.

How does the exact first-order count work?

In the θ = 0 proxy, each branch of the rotation, Z on a subset of the rotated qubits, has a symptom: the detectors it lights. A single fault survives post-selection if and only if its symptom equals a branch symptom. The count enumerates every fault in the proxy's error model, keeps the ones that match, weights each by the closed-form failure weight of its branch pair, and sums. It reproduced 665 million sampled shots with no fitted parameter, and then predicted a blind test.

What is pre-registration, and why use it?

Writing the hypothesis, the predictions and the pass/fail criteria into a protocol and committing it before running. It stops the analysis from being adjusted to fit the data. A guard checks the git dates, so a result can't silently predate its protocol.

Session 13 · 20 min

The project, in plain words

The question. QuEra's papers describe noise with one knob, p, and turn every channel up or down together. A hardware team has to choose which error to improve next. So: which hardware error controls STAR's injection error, and what would halving each one buy?

What it did, in four steps

  1. Reproduced QuEra's reference curve (P01). It only matched with a decoder that drops hyperedges (Session 5), and its large-angle part was mostly memory errors read out after the rotation, not injection. So the project measures the injection error alone: noise only around the rotation, every detector post-selected, no decoder.
  2. Rebuilt the circuits with Table II noise (P02). Each hardware error placed on the right gate. Checked first: with equal noise everywhere, the rebuild reproduces QuEra's own error model exactly. Then 665 million sampled shots showed the injection error is one constant times the misrotation shape fk(θ) from π/4 to π/128.
  3. Counted it exactly (P03). The first-order count of Session 12 reproduced all of that sampling with no fitted parameter.
  4. Tested it blind (P04). Predictions for each hardware channel alone, a halved channel, a new angle, and distance 7 were committed before sampling. All nine fell within 0.95–1.10 of the prediction, including d = 7 (2.60 × 10−4 sampled against 2.53 × 10−4 predicted).

The answer

Who pays for STAR's injection error at d = 5, θ = π/16, Table II scaled to p = 10−3 (computed, and confirmed by the blind test). Right of each bar: what halving that channel buys in simulation volume.
  • Single-qubit gates are the biggest share, about half. But the rotation gate itself is only 12–16%. The rest is the Hadamards that turning each CNOT into a CZ leaves on the data qubits. Any Z that lands on a rotated qubit before its first check hides, whichever gate caused it.
  • Spectators and the CZ carry about a quarter each.
  • Initialization, measurement and loss cost nothing at first order. Each lights a detector no branch lights, so post-selection removes it. They cost acceptance (more retries), not accuracy.
  • The CZ's correlated ZZ error, its largest term, is free here, because the ancilla half always flips that ancilla's measurement.
  • Halving single-qubit-gate error buys ×1.32 in simulation volume (sampled: ×1.36).

What is new, and what isn't

Counting undetected single faults at first order is not new: the original STAR paper (Toshio et al.) and Choi et al. did it, Liu et al. counted error-model faults for Clifford injection, and Veroni et al. describe the window in which faults near the rotation hide. What is new here is narrower: extending the error-model count to any angle, running it channel by channel over QuEra's real circuits, and testing it blind.

What it does not show

  • These are not QuEra's own protocol. They are the challenge's teaching circuits, which rotate the whole column (k = d) after one round. The paper's protocol uses k = 3 with extra rounds. The method carries over; applying it to that protocol is the next step.
  • The α here is about six times the paper's. It is 8.6–9.1 here, against about 1.5 for the paper's own protocol. The two differ in k, in the rounds before the rotation, and in the rotation gate, and which of these accounts for the gap has not been tested. So the two numbers are not compared directly.
  • “ZZ is free” is narrow. It holds for the syndrome CZs around a single-qubit rotation. If the rotation itself is a two-qubit RZZ, a ZZ there is exactly a branch flip, the critical fault (Veroni et al.).
  • It is an idealized injection error: every detector post-selected, later rounds noiseless, no decoder. A real protocol decodes some faults that this metric rejects.
  • Loss is modelled the way Table II models it, as a Y error. Real atom loss isn't a Pauli error, and is not priced here.
  • First order is an approximation: about 1% low at p = 10⁻³, and 10% low at full Table II strength, where it was measured.
In one breath

For QuEra's STAR circuits under Table II noise, single-qubit gates cause about half the injection error, mostly through the Hadamards that CZ compilation leaves on the data; spectators and the CZ share the rest; initialization, measurement and loss cost only retries; and an exact first-order count, tested blind, predicts all of it.

Which hardware error limits STAR in this study, and how sure is the result?

Single-qubit gates, about half the injection error at d = 5, mostly from the Hadamards that CX-to-CZ compilation leaves on data qubits. Why do initialization, measurement and loss cost nothing?

At first order, each of those faults lights a detector that no rotation branch lights, so post-selection always discards it. They lower the acceptance rate, meaning more retries, but they don't change the error of the states you keep. Sampling found a handful of two-fault events, all below 0.7% of the total.

What was the biggest surprise in the project?

The very first experiment failed: the pipeline didn't reproduce QuEra's reference curve. That's why the project measures the injection error alone, and it led to the tsim issue and pull request.
